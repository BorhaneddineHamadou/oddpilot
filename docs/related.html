Where odd-pilot sits in the field

A tool that claims "nobody checks whether scenarios are physically possible" owes the field a careful comparison. This page examines the systems closest to odd-pilot — what each one does, what it deliberately does not do, what we add on top, and, just as honestly, what each does better than us. Every factual claim links to its source; corrections are welcome as issues.

How to read this comparison

odd-pilot combines four capabilities that usually live in different tools. For each related system we ask which of these it provides:

AxisThe question it answersIn odd-pilot
A — Artifact validityIs this existing scenario file physically possible and internally consistent?physcheck L0–L4, 157 cited rules
B — Constructive realismAre newly generated scenarios realistic by construction?BN sampling + the physcheck gate in plan
C — Coverage adequacyHave we tested enough of expected real operation, with a defensible stopping rule?PWCC in assess
D — ODD linkageDoes testing respect the declared operating envelope?L5 + conform

Being the only tool that combines A–D is an integration claim, not a claim that each axis is unprecedented — the sections below give each lineage its due.

ASAM Quality Checker Framework

Official open-source checker framework for the OpenX standards · github.com/asam-ev/qc-framework · asam.net

What it does
A framework of "checker bundles" for ASAM formats, with official bundles for OpenDRIVE and OpenSCENARIO XML. The OpenSCENARIO bundle (~16 checkers) validates XML well-formedness, schema compliance, resolvable references (entities, variables, storyboard elements), parameter declarations and basic value constraints (e.g. non-negative transition times). Extensible: anyone can add bundles in any language, results merge into one report.
What it does not check
Anything beyond formal correctness: no physical plausibility, no weather consistency, no map cross-checks, no kinematics — its own documentation scopes it to structure, schema and references. In physcheck's terms, it covers roughly layer L0.
What we add
The physics: 157 cited rules across environment (L1), map and solar geometry (L2), motion feasibility (L3), storyboard logic (L4), ODD (L5) and statistical realism (L6) — plus the benchmark that measures the checker's own false-positive rate on real-world-derived files, which the QC framework has no equivalent of.
What it does better
Standardisation and neutrality: it is ASAM's own framework, multi-format (OpenDRIVE too), with an ecosystem process behind it. The natural convergence — on our roadmap — is packaging physcheck as a QC checker bundle, so its physics rules run inside the official framework.

Scenic / VerifAI

Probabilistic scenario programming language (UC Berkeley / UCSC) · docs.scenic-lang.org · Fremont et al.

What it does
Scenarios are programs: distributions over scene configurations and behaviors, with hard and soft constraints. Sampling yields concrete scenarios that respect the constraints by construction — arguably the most elegant prevention of implausibility for scenarios authored in Scenic. VerifAI adds falsification and analysis around it.
What it does not check
Anything it did not generate. The OpenSCENARIO 1.x ecosystem — hand-written suites, dataset conversions, vendor catalogs, template libraries (where we found the 200 m/s² placeholders and midnight suns) — is outside its reach. And the realism of Scenic output is bounded by what each programmer chose to constrain: there is no built-in, citation-grounded physics catalog; nothing stops an author declaring rain with a friction of 1.0 unless they wrote that constraint themselves.
What we add
Post-hoc, artifact-level auditing with an explicit, cited rule catalog — validity as a checkable property of the file, not of the generating process. The two compose naturally: Scenic-generated scenarios exported to OpenSCENARIO can be physchecked like any other file.
What it does better
Expressing scenario intent and structured randomness; multi-simulator execution; a mature academic ecosystem. For authoring new scenario families from scratch, a constraint language beats a linter.

The PEGASUS family (PEGASUS, 6-layer model, scenario databases)

German flagship methodology projects for ADS release · scenario database · 6-layer model · method overview

What it does
The process backbone of scenario-based testing: functional → logical → concrete scenario abstraction, the 6-layer environment model (road, infrastructure, temporary changes, objects, environment, digital information), scenario databases fed from real traffic, criticality metrics, and database-level quality criteria (input-data quality, concept completeness, processing quality, database coverage).
What it does not check
PEGASUS's quality criteria govern the database and its pipeline, not individual scenario artifacts: there is no per-file physics validation of the OpenSCENARIO instances a database exports, no cited-threshold catalog, and no released open checker. Its coverage notion is database completeness, not a risk-calibrated exposure stopping rule.
What we add
The enforcement layer PEGASUS-style processes assume but do not ship: machine-checkable physics at the artifact level (our layers loosely instantiate the 6-layer model's road/environment/object separation), plus PWCC's quantified adequacy with an explicit ε-stopping decision.
What it does better
Institutional scope: data pipelines, criticality analysis, release argumentation, regulator engagement — a process odd-pilot plugs into rather than replaces.

Foretellix / OpenSCENARIO 2.0 DSL

Industrial coverage-driven verification; M-SDL seeded OSC 2.0 · foretellix.com · M-SDL → OSC 2.0

What it does
Brings hardware-verification discipline to ADS testing: abstract scenarios with constraints, mass concretisation, execution monitoring, and measurable coverage goals ("quality of coverage over quantity of miles") with predefined verification suites, KPIs and checkers. The most mature industrial realisation of coverage-driven scenario testing.
What it does not check
Its coverage is defined over user-declared parameter spaces and goals — reaching your own coverage targets says nothing about how much of real-world operation those targets represent, and there is no published probability-weighted link to field data with a statistical stopping rule. Runtime checkers judge behaviour, not the declared physics of the input files; it is commercial and closed; and it centres on OSC 2.0, not the vast existing OSC 1.x artifact base.
What we add
Open tooling for the 1.x world; coverage weighted by a learned operational distribution with exposure requirements derived from a risk budget (Emin = −ln α · P̃(c)/ρ) rather than hand-declared goals; and artifact-level physics auditing with a benchmarked false-positive rate.
What it does better
Scale, execution orchestration, industrial hardening, behavioural checkers at runtime, and OSC 2.0 leadership. In a mature deployment the two are complementary: Foretellix-style campaigns are exactly where an operational-weighted adequacy metric and an input-physics gate would slot in.

CommonRoad Drivability Checker

Open toolbox for motion-planning validation (TUM) · docs · IV 2020 paper

What it does
Validates planned trajectories against vehicle models: collision checking, road-boundary compliance, and kinematic feasibility by reconstructing the control inputs a trajectory would require and checking them against the vehicle model's constraints. Real-time capable; the reference for feasibility checking in the motion-planning community.
What it does not check
It validates a planner's output in the CommonRoad format — not test scenarios as artifacts: no OpenSCENARIO, no environment physics (weather, friction chains, solar geometry), no storyboard logic, no notion of a test campaign.
What we add
The same spirit applied one level up: our L3 friction-circle and trajectory checks ask "could any real vehicle do what this scenario declares?", composed with the L1 environment (braking bounds shrink on a flooded road) and embedded in a full campaign toolchain for the OpenSCENARIO ecosystem.
What it does better
Model fidelity: it checks against parametrised vehicle dynamics models, where our L3 uses deliberately generous cited envelopes. For validating a specific vehicle's planned motion, use CommonRoad; for auditing declared scenarios, physcheck.

TNO StreetWise & scenario-database coverage metrics

Data-driven scenario mining and representativeness assessment · StreetWise · coverage metrics (de Gelder et al.) · representativeness metric

What it does
Mines scenarios from real road data and — closest in spirit to our assess — quantifies whether a scenario database covers the ODD and the driving data, using density estimation over scenario parameters (KDE/SVD) and representativeness metrics for generated parameters.
What it does not check
The object of assessment is the database: is the collection representative? It does not compute per-combination required exposure from a risk budget, has no adequacy/stopping verdict for an executed campaign, no physics validation of exported artifacts, and no generation loop closing measured gaps.
What we add
The step from "the suite is representative" to "the executed testing is enough": PWCC ties each condition combination's required hours to its real-world probability and a risk budget, yields an explicit STOP/CONTINUE verdict, and feeds its gaps directly into gated generation.
What it does better
Scenario mining from raw fleet data — the upstream step we deliberately don't do (our model fit starts from a tabular profile someone else extracted). A StreetWise-style pipeline is the natural producer of odd-pilot's profiling input.

Situation coverage (Alexander, Hawkins & Rae)

Coverage criterion for autonomous-robot testing (York, 2015) · paper · AV case study

What it does
The founding articulation of coverage over external situations rather than code: enumerate a situation space, measure which combinations testing has visited, drive generation toward unvisited ones.
What it does not check
Combinations count equally regardless of how often reality produces them, a visit counts regardless of exposure duration, and there is no risk-calibrated threshold saying when coverage suffices — the authors themselves note the epistemic gap. No validity checking of the situations either.
What we add
PWCC is situation coverage made operational-statistical: mass-weighted by a learned distribution, exposure-based (hours, not visits), with Emin derived from confidence and risk budget — plus the guarantee that generated situations are physically possible at all.
What it does better
Simplicity and generality: it needs no operational data and applies to any autonomous system, which is why it remains the standard baseline — and it is the intellectual ancestor our metric should be evaluated against.

Ontology-based scene & scenario generation

Knowledge-based generation (Bagschik, Menzel & Maurer; successors) · IV 2018 paper

What it does
Encodes traffic-domain expert knowledge in an ontology and generates scenes/scenarios that respect the encoded relations — consistency by construction, the knowledge-engineering sibling of Scenic's constraint approach; feeds functional-scenario catalogs for safety analysis.
What it does not check
Existing artifacts: like Scenic, correctness lives in the generator, not in an auditable property of the files the rest of the world produces. Published ontologies centre on road/traffic structure; quantitative environmental physics (friction-temperature- precipitation couplings, solar geometry, tire limits) with literature-cited thresholds is not their focus.
What we add
A complementary enforcement point (audit the artifact, whoever made it) and the quantitative physics layer with citations — our rule catalog is, in effect, a small quantified ontology of physical couplings, with a benchmark measuring its own error rate.
What it does better
Rich structural/semantic relations (road topology, traffic rules, actor roles) beyond what attribute-level predicates express; a principled path to completeness arguments over scenario catalogs.

Test-input validity research (DL testing)

SE research on invalid generated test inputs · ICSE 2023 empirical study · active-learning validators

What it does
Established, in the deep-learning testing domain, the exact problem pattern we target: test generators produce inputs outside the valid domain, invalid inputs yield spurious failures and false confidence, and automated validators are needed. Validators there are typically learned (VAE reconstruction error, discriminators) or human-in-the-loop, because "is this a digit?" resists formalisation.
What it does not check
Driving scenarios. And its learned validators are probabilistic and opaque — appropriate for images, unnecessary for a domain with physical laws.
What we add
The observation that for ADS scenarios validity is largely formalisable: physics gives symbolic, citable, deterministic validity predicates — no learned validator needed for the impossible, with the learned tier (L6) reserved for the merely-improbable. Our generated-corpus study (98.6–100 % invalid) is the ADS-scenario analogue of their empirical finding, with a stronger oracle.
What it does better
Domains without formalisable validity — and a decade of framing on validity-vs-usefulness of generated tests that our papers should build on explicitly.

ODD standards: ISO 34503, BSI PAS 1883, ASAM OpenODD

Taxonomies and formats for declaring the operating envelope · ISO 34503:2023

What they do
Define how to state an ODD: hierarchical taxonomies of scenery, environment and dynamic-element conditions (ISO 34503, PAS 1883) and a machine-readable exchange format (OpenODD concept). The standards are explicit that test procedures and runtime monitoring are out of scope.
What they do not provide
An executable conformance check for test artifacts — a declared ODD with no tool that asks "does this scenario suite actually stay inside it?" remains paperwork.
What we add
conform operationalises the include/exclude semantics per scenario with in/out/undeclared verdicts, and assess weighs coverage within that envelope. (Honest caveat: our ODD YAML implements OpenODD's semantics over our attribute vocabulary — it does not yet parse the ASAM exchange format itself.)
What they do better
They are the normative reference — our job is to track them, not replace them.

So what is genuinely ours?

  • Artifact-level physics auditing with citations. The official ASAM checker validates form; generation languages prevent what their authors encode; nothing else audits the OpenSCENARIO 1.x artifact base — hand-written, converted, catalogued — against literature-grounded physics. That gap is confirmed by the checkers' own scope statements, and by what we found in the public suites.
  • A checker that measures its own error rate. We found no comparable tool with a real-data false-positive benchmark and adversarial triage of every finding. This is standard practice in some fields; it should be here.
  • Risk-calibrated, operationally-weighted adequacy. Coverage notions exist (situation coverage, Foretellix goals, database representativeness); tying required exposure per combination to a learned operational distribution and a risk budget, with an explicit stopping verdict, is PWCC's contribution (paper in submission) — odd-pilot is its first embedding in a full toolchain.
  • The closed loop with a validity gate. Generate from the measured gaps, keep dependencies naturalistic by conditioning the operational model, and physics-check every candidate before it exists — quantified in our study: 98.6–100 % invalid for standard strategies vs 0 % gated, at 99 % yield.
And what we owe the field: the situation-coverage line gave us the question; PEGASUS the abstraction levels and layer thinking; Scenic and the ontologies the proof that constructive realism works at authoring time; CommonRoad the standard for kinematic feasibility checking; the DL-testing community the validity-of-generated-inputs framing; TNO the data-driven coverage spirit; ASAM the formats that make any of this interoperable. odd-pilot is an integration and enforcement layer standing on all of it.
